Research guide

TB-500

What the evidence says about this thymosin beta-4 fragment, and what it does not

27 November 2026

What it is

TB-500 is a name used for a product marketed as a fragment of thymosin beta-4. An analytical paper found an acetylated seven-amino-acid fragment of thymosin beta-4 in a sample sold under this name. That fragment is not the same molecule as full-length thymosin beta-4.

The name on a label does not by itself tell you what is inside another sample. The analytical paper identified contents in one sample, and a separate equine study looked at detecting the fragment and its metabolites in horse samples. Neither paper shows a human healing outcome, how often side effects occur, long-term safety, or human-use instructions.

[1] [2]

1. The studies at a glance

The evidence supplied here comes from two papers with different aims. One is an analytical identification of a sample marketed as TB-500. The other is an equine study of detection in horse samples.

Neither study compares treatment with a control in people. So the at-a-glance view is about what was measured, not about a human treatment result.

2. Forms and handling

The analytical paper describes an acetylated seven-amino-acid fragment found in a marketed sample. That is a statement about one sample, not a specification for every product sold under the name.

A clear liquid or a commercial label does not establish what is present. Because product names alone do not identify contents, the only reliable description of a given sample is the one that comes from testing that sample.

[1]

3. Side effects

The two cited papers do not report side-effect frequency. They also do not report long-term safety. Any statement about how often something occurs in people therefore goes beyond what these studies measured.

For severe allergy signs, the supplied emergency information is generic. Watch for swelling of the face, lips, tongue or throat, a widespread rash, wheezing or trouble breathing, and seek urgent care if these appear.

4. How it has been studied

The first study is analytical. It examined a sample marketed as TB-500 and identified an acetylated seven-amino-acid fragment of thymosin beta-4. The finding is about identity, and it shows the fragment is distinct from the full-length protein.

The second study is in horses. It examined detection of the fragment and its metabolites in equine samples. This measured detection, not treatment of tendon pain or injury. The two designs answer different questions, and neither is a human clinical trial.

[1] [2]

5. What the evidence actually shows

The evidence shows that a marketed sample contained a specific fragment, and that the fragment and its metabolites can be detected in horse samples. It does not show that the product heals human injuries or relieves human tendon pain.

It also does not establish human side-effect rates, long-term safety, or human-use instructions. Those are separate questions that the cited papers leave open. The gap is narrow and specific rather than a claim that no human research of any form exists anywhere.

[1] [2]

6. Who should be cautious

Anyone with persistent injury symptoms should have them evaluated by a qualified professional rather than assuming a marketed product explains or resolves them. That advice follows from the fact that the cited studies did not measure a human treatment effect.

People with known allergies should also be alert to the generic severe allergy signs, because the papers do not describe side-effect patterns in people.

[1] [2] [3]

7. Monitoring

The cited studies do not define a monitoring plan for people. The analytical paper monitored identity in a sample, and the equine paper monitored detection in horses. Neither sets out what a person should track.

In practice, the useful anchors from the evidence are the identity of what is being used and the persistence of injury symptoms. Beyond that, the papers are silent.

[1] [2]

8. Food and muscle

The supplied evidence does not address food, meals or muscle effects. The equine study measured detection in horse samples, not muscle performance or recovery.

So the honest position is that the cited papers say nothing about food timing and nothing about muscle outcomes in people. Any link between the fragment and muscle belongs to evidence outside this fact sheet.

[2]

9. Timing and interruptions

The papers do not provide a human schedule, so they cannot say what to do about a missed dose. What they do support is a distinction between detection and effect. Being detectable in a horse sample is not the same as producing a human benefit.

Because no human timing is supplied, timing guidance from a seller should be treated as separate from the study findings rather than as something the papers established.

[1] [2]

10. Common mistakes

The most common mistake is treating the name as proof of contents. The analytical paper shows that a sample contained a fragment, but product names alone do not identify other samples.

A second mistake is reading the equine detection study as a treatment trial. It measured detection, not tendon pain or injury outcomes. A third is assuming the papers establish safety frequency or long-term safety, which they do not.

11. FAQ

Is TB-500 the same as thymosin beta-4?
No. The analytical paper identified an acetylated seven-amino-acid fragment of thymosin beta-4, and the fragment is distinct from the full-length molecule.
Does the label tell me what is in the product?
Not on its own. Product names alone do not identify the contents of other samples, so the label is not a substitute for testing.
Was the horse study a treatment trial?
It examined detection of the fragment and its metabolites in horse samples. It measured detection, not treatment of tendon pain or injury.
Do the papers prove it works in people?
They do not demonstrate a human healing outcome. They also leave open side-effect frequency, long-term safety and human-use instructions.
