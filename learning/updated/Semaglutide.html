Research guide

Semaglutide

What the trial evidence shows, where it stops, and what to check before you rely on it

27 November 2026

What it is

Semaglutide is a prescription medicine that activates the GLP-1 receptor. That receptor action can increase insulin release when blood glucose is high, affect appetite and slow gastric emptying. It comes as distinct prescription injection and oral tablet products, and the products carry different directions.

Because the products differ, the exact product matters more than the name on the box. Confirm which product you have with the prescriber or pharmacist, then follow the instructions supplied with that product. The evidence below describes what was studied, not a personal forecast.

[3]

1. The studies at a glance

Two large randomised trials anchor the evidence, and they asked different questions. STEP 1 tested weight change in adults with overweight or obesity and no diabetes. SELECT tested cardiovascular outcomes in adults with overweight or obesity and established cardiovascular disease, also without diabetes.

Both trials compared semaglutide injection with placebo, and both gave lifestyle intervention to both groups. Neither trial answers questions about the oral tablet, about people with diabetes, or about how a single person will respond.

2. Forms and handling

The injection and the tablet are separate prescription products with separate directions. Handling guidance for one does not transfer to the other, so read the instructions for the exact product in hand and ask a pharmacist or prescriber when something is unclear.

The supplied evidence does not describe storage, device technique or tablet handling in detail. That is a narrow gap, and the product's own instructions fill it better than any general summary can.

[3]

3. Side effects

Digestive effects are the ones the evidence names, including nausea, diarrhea, vomiting and constipation. These are common enough to plan around, and they often shape how tolerable the medicine feels early on.

Some symptoms deserve prompt care rather than patience. Persistent severe abdominal pain, or repeated vomiting that prevents you staying hydrated, should be checked quickly. Trouble breathing, swelling of the throat or tongue, or collapse are emergencies.

When to seek urgent care

  • Persistent severe abdominal pain
  • Repeated vomiting that prevents you staying hydrated
  • Trouble breathing
  • Swelling of the throat or tongue
  • Collapse

4. How it has been studied

STEP 1 enrolled 1,961 adults with overweight or obesity and no diabetes. Everyone received lifestyle intervention, and the trial ran to 68 weeks. Mean weight change was a fall of 14.9 percent with semaglutide injection against 2.4 percent with placebo.

SELECT enrolled 17,604 adults with overweight or obesity and established cardiovascular disease, again without diabetes. Over a mean 39.8 months, the composite of death from cardiovascular causes, nonfatal heart attack or nonfatal stroke occurred in 6.5 percent with semaglutide and 8.0 percent with placebo.

[1] [2]

5. What the evidence actually shows

In the studied injection, the evidence shows meaningful average weight loss in adults with overweight or obesity and no diabetes, and fewer cardiovascular composite events in adults who already had cardiovascular disease. Both findings come from randomised comparisons against placebo with lifestyle intervention in both groups.

The limits are as important as the findings. Averages describe groups, not individuals, and the results should not be generalised to all patients or to other formulations. Body weight alone also does not describe strength or body composition.

[1] [2]

6. Who should be cautious

The trials studied adults with overweight or obesity, and SELECT specifically required established cardiovascular disease. People with diabetes were not in either trial, so the evidence here says little about them directly.

Anyone managing digestive symptoms, diabetes medicines or blood sugar monitoring should discuss tolerance with a care team. The supplied evidence does not list other groups to avoid, which is a narrow gap rather than an all-clear.

7. Monitoring

The evidence supports watching digestive tolerance and staying alert to the urgent symptoms above. For people also managing diabetes medicines and blood sugar, the care team is the right place to discuss monitoring.

The supplied material does not specify a monitoring schedule or which tests to expect. Follow the instructions for the exact product and raise changes with a prescriber or pharmacist.

[3] [4]

8. Food and muscle

Semaglutide slows gastric emptying, which helps explain why digestive effects feature so prominently. Both trials paired the medicine with lifestyle intervention, so the results reflect that combination rather than the medicine alone.

Weight change figures do not tell you how much of any loss was fat or muscle. Judging strength and body composition needs more than a scale, and the supplied evidence does not quantify it.

[1] [2] [3]

9. Timing and interruptions

Timing rules differ between the injection and the tablet, so there is no single answer that covers both. The exact product's instructions are the place to check, and a prescriber is the person to ask about interruptions.

The supplied evidence gives no missed-dose windows, starting steps or maximums. Treat any general timing advice as a prompt to read your own product's directions rather than a substitute for them.

[3]

10. Common mistakes

The most common mistake is treating the injection and the tablet as interchangeable. They are distinct products with different directions, so technique and timing borrowed from the other form can mislead.

Two more mistakes stand out. Reading a group average as a personal forecast ignores how wide individual responses are. Waiting too long on severe abdominal pain or repeated vomiting that blocks hydration delays care that the symptom pattern warrants.

11. FAQ

Does this guide tell me my dose?
No. It describes what was studied. The exact product's instructions and a pharmacist or prescriber cover the practical detail.
Can I apply the trial results to the tablet?
Only cautiously. Both trials used the injection, so the tablet is outside what these findings directly tested.
Do the results apply if I have diabetes?
Not directly. Neither trial enrolled people with diabetes, so the evidence here is silent on that group.
Is the weight figure a prediction for me?
No. It is a group average from one trial, and individual responses vary widely around it.

12. Plain-English glossary

Placebo
A comparison treatment with no active medicine, used so researchers can see what the medicine adds.
Composite outcome
A single measure that counts the first of several events, such as death from cardiovascular causes, heart attack or stroke.
Gastric emptying
How quickly the stomach passes its contents onward, which semaglutide slows.
Randomised trial
A study where participants are assigned by chance to one treatment or another, which makes the groups fairer to compare.
