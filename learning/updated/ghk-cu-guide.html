Research peptide guide · plain language

GHK-Cu

Concentrations, mixing, side effects and what the evidence actually supports

Prepared 27 November 2026 · cosmetic ingredient, injectable form unapproved

What it is

GHK-Cu is a copper tripeptide complex. The peptide is three amino acids — glycine, histidine, lysine — with a copper ion bound to it. It occurs naturally in human plasma and is present in wound fluid.

In the lab it does a lot: it stimulates collagen and glycosaminoglycan production, calms inflammation, acts as an antioxidant, and switches gene expression. That breadth is why it appears in so many serums. The human evidence, though, is one small trial in one procedure setting, and it is topical.

The distinction that runs through this whole document. Topical GHK-Cu is a cosmetic ingredient with one supportive trial. Injectable GHK-Cu has no trial behind it at all — not one. Every injectable number below is convention borrowed from cosmetic dosing and community practice. Keep the two apart or you will end up treating a guess as a finding.

1. Dosing

Topical — the only route with a trial

The published study used a whole topical regimen containing GHK-Cu, applied after laser resurfacing around the mouth, assessed at twelve weeks. It does not report a percentage, and it tested a regimen rather than an isolated ingredient, so no single concentration can be lifted out of it.

What commercial products actually use, for orientation:

Use twice daily on clean, dry skin — face, neck, hands, or a healing site. A pea-sized amount is enough for the face. More product is not more effect.

Injectable — convention only

No trial exists. Practice has settled around 1–2 mg subcutaneously, daily or every other day, in cycles of four to eight weeks. Some go to 3 mg. The shape of it:

What I would suggest as a starting shape

  • Topical first. Cheaper, evidenced, and the route the trial used. Start at 0.1% twice daily for a week, then 0.5% if skin is calm. Reserve 1% for skin that has already tolerated 0.5%.
  • Injectable, if you add it: 1 mg daily for the first week, then 1–2 mg daily or every other day.
  • Cycle: six to eight weeks, then two to four weeks off.
  • Stop rule: no visible change to skin, hair or a healing site after eight weeks at a full dose means stop rather than escalate.
Worked example. Priya, 68 kg, wants it for post-procedure healing on the face. Week 1: 0.1% serum twice daily on the treated area. Week 2 onward: 0.5% twice daily. If she later adds injections, 1 mg daily puts her at 14.7 mcg/kg, mid-band, and stays there for six weeks.

2. Mixing

A. Injectable reconstitution

Same mechanics as any lyophilised peptide. Clean surface, slow hands, fifteen minutes.

  • Your vial of powder
  • Bacteriostatic water
  • One 5 mL mixing syringe, 25–27G
  • Alcohol swabs
  • U-100 insulin syringes, 29–31G
  • Sharps container and marker pen
  1. Wash your hands for twenty seconds; dry on a clean towel; wipe the table.
  2. Check the vial — compact cake, legible label, in date.
  3. Swab both stoppers, separate swabs, ten seconds each, and let them dry fully.
  4. Draw the water — air in, invert, draw slowly, clear the bubbles.
  5. Change the needle if you have a spare.
  6. Enter at 45 degrees against the inner wall, then straighten.
  7. Run the water down the glass, not onto the powder.
  8. Vent the vacuum by re-pulling to your mark and holding until it eases.
  9. Withdraw and cap.
  10. Swirl, never shake. GHK-Cu solutions are deep blue when correct — the colour is the copper.
  11. Stand five to fifteen minutes, swirling occasionally, until fully dissolved.
  12. Check it. Clear deep blue is right. Cloudy, or a faded grey-blue, means something is off.
  13. Label with date, concentration and dose units.
  14. Refrigerate at 2–8°C, dark. Never freeze.
The colour is diagnostic. Deep blue is intact GHK-Cu. A solution that fades toward grey, green-grey or pale pink has lost its copper and degraded — do not use it. This is a more useful check than for most peptides, because you can see it.

B. Making a topical serum

Straightforward arithmetic, but the units catch people. 1% = 10 mg per mL.

Target strengthPowder per 30 mLPowder per 100 mL
0.1%30 mg100 mg
0.5%150 mg500 mg
1%300 mg1000 mg
  1. Choose a base that suits copper. A simple hydrating serum or cream at pH 5.5–6.5. Avoid a base that is strongly acidic.
  2. Weigh the powder on a 0.01 g scale. Weighing beats scooping — a quarter-teaspoon is not a measurement.
  3. Wet the powder with a few drops of base and let it sit for a minute. It dissolves to deep blue.
  4. Add the rest of the base and stir gently until uniform, with no grit.
  5. Check the colour — even deep blue throughout. Streaks mean it is not mixed.
  6. Decant into a dark or opaque pump bottle.
  7. Label with strength and date. Refrigerate; use within four to six weeks.

3. Side effects

No safety rate exists for either route. The trial was thirteen people and tracked satisfaction and skin measures, not adverse events. What follows is reported experience.

Topical — reported

  • Stinging or tingling on application, especially on broken or freshly resurfaced skin
  • Redness and warmth that settles within the hour
  • Itching
  • Dryness or tightness
  • A temporary breakout or purge in the first two weeks, mostly in acne-prone skin
  • Contact dermatitis with repeated use at high strength
  • Blue-green staining of pale fabric, pillows and towels — cosmetic, not a side effect, but it happens

Injectable — reported

  • Injection-site redness, stinging or a small lump
  • Nausea, usually mild
  • Headache
  • Dizziness or light-headedness shortly after injecting
  • Fatigue
  • Flushing or a warm feeling
  • Transient aching at an injury site

Theoretical or less certain

  • Because GHK-Cu promotes blood vessel growth, whether that matters in anyone with an active cancer history — unanswered
  • Copper sensitivity in people who react to copper jewellery — plausible, untested
  • Copper accumulation at these doses is not considered a concern, but nobody has measured it
  • Long-term use, beyond a few months, is undocumented
  • Purity varies by supplier and is not verified

Serious — stop and get help

  • Hives, lip or tongue swelling, throat tightness, trouble breathing, collapse
  • Swelling or pain at an injection site that keeps growing
  • Fever or spreading redness suggesting infection
  • A rash that spreads beyond the application area

Reducing irritation: drop the strength, apply to damp skin, buffer with a plain moisturiser over the top, and never apply to broken skin at 1% or above.

4. Applying and injecting

Topical

  • Clean, dry skin; a pea-sized amount for the face
  • Pat, do not rub hard
  • Let it absorb before the next layer
  • Twice daily; morning is the better slot, since acids usually go at night
  • Keep it off the eyelids and out of the eyes

Injectable

  • Abdomen two fingers clear of the navel, or front thigh
  • Swab and let it dry; pinch a fold; 90 degrees, or 45 if lean
  • Inject slowly; count to five before withdrawing
  • Rotate sites; do not rub

5. What the evidence actually shows

The trial was randomised and controlled, which is the right design. It followed carbon dioxide laser resurfacing around the mouth, and thirteen people completed the two topical-regimen arms.

At twelve weeks, satisfaction favoured the GHK-Cu regimen. Objective evaluations did not find a significant between-group difference in redness resolution, wrinkles or overall skin quality.

Both halves matter. People liked it more; the measurements did not separate it from the comparison regimen. That is a satisfaction signal, not a demonstrated objective advantage — and it sits inside one procedure, one clinic, thirteen people.

Outside that, the evidence is in vitro and animal: fibroblast collagen production, wound closure in rats, anti-inflammatory markers. Broad and consistent, and not human.

6. Regulatory status

Topically, GHK-Cu is a cosmetic ingredient — sold without approval, regulated for safety rather than efficacy, and not permitted to claim it treats anything.

Injectable GHK-Cu is not an approved drug anywhere and has no trial behind it. In the United States, compounding pharmacies have used it under enforcement discretion; the FDA has not placed it on the list of substances that may routinely be compounded.

Practical consequence: the label is only as good as the supplier, and a topical percentage is a marketing number before it is a pharmacological one.

7. Who should be cautious

  • Anyone with known copper sensitivity or a reaction to copper jewellery
  • Pregnancy or breastfeeding — unstudied
  • Active cancer or recent history — theoretical concern about vessel growth
  • Eczema, rosacea or a damaged skin barrier — start very low
  • Freshly resurfaced or wounded skin — ask the treating clinician first
  • Wilson's disease or any copper-handling disorder
  • Age under 18

8. Monitoring

  • Photograph the area in the same light weekly — memory is unreliable for skin
  • Note stinging, redness duration and whether it settles
  • Track breakouts; a purge should settle by week three, irritation will not
  • For injections, check sites for lumps or spreading redness
  • Score the actual complaint weekly, not the product's reputation

9. Food and muscle

The trial covers neither. GHK-Cu is not a muscle drug and does not build tissue on its own — it is a signalling peptide, and signalling needs material to work with.

For skin, that means protein, adequate calories, sleep and sun protection. For a healing site, it means not picking at it. Topical GHK-Cu on a face that gets no sunscreen is a rounding error.

10. Timing and interruptions

Nothing established. The trial assessed at twelve weeks and gives no application schedule beyond the regimen itself. Twice daily is convention. Missed applications are inconsequential; resume when you remember. There is no described rebound from stopping.

11. Common mistakes

  • Treating the topical result as proof for injections
  • Assuming one product speaks for all formulations
  • Reading the satisfaction result as an objective win and skipping the null objective finding
  • Layering it over vitamin C or an acid and degrading the peptide
  • Scooping powder instead of weighing it
  • Jumping straight to 1% and calling the resulting sting an allergy
  • Using a clear bottle and letting light destroy it
  • Expecting a wrinkle result in two weeks
  • Buying blue-dyed product and mistaking dye for copper
  • Assuming purity because the powder is blue

12. FAQ

Does GHK-Cu work?
People in the one trial liked it more. Objective measures did not separate it from the comparison. Partial at best.
Can I use the topical results for injections?
No. Different route, no absorption data, no injectable trial at all.
What strength should a serum be?
Commercial products run 0.05–0.5%. Start at 0.1% and build to 0.5%; 1% is the ceiling for most people.
What dose do people inject?
1–2 mg daily or every other day, four to eight weeks. Convention only.
Does my weight change the dose?
No, essentially. The spread across adult weights is about twofold.
Why is it blue?
The bound copper. Fading colour means the copper is coming off and the peptide is degrading.
Can I use it with vitamin C?
Not in the same layer. Low pH strips the copper. Separate them by time of day.
Is it safe long term?
Unknown. Nobody has run it long enough in enough people.
Will it help hair?
It is popular for scalp use and the mechanism is plausible. Human hair data is thin.
