What the video says

Dr Kidd, an Australian general practitioner, uses this 4.5 minute video to explain CagriSema and compare it with two existing GLP-1 based weight loss drugs. He says he has helped thousands of patients with weight loss, and he frames the video around whether CagriSema is the best of the three.

He first describes the background. Semaglutide mimics the hormone GLP1, and tirzepatide is a dual GLP1 and GIP agonist. He then presents CagriSema as a different kind of combination: semaglutide plus cagrilintide, which he describes as a newer compound acting on amylin and calcitonin receptors. He says amylin slows how fast food leaves the stomach and sends satiety signals to the brain, and that calcitonin, usually known for calcium and bone regulation, also affects appetite through brain receptors.

The middle section is a results comparison. For semaglutide at 2.4 mg he cites average weight loss of about 15% after roughly a year. For tirzepatide at 15 mg he cites about 20% at 72 weeks, and he explains the gap between 22.5% (without dropouts) and 20.9% (with dropouts), choosing 20.9% as his conservative figure. For CagriSema he describes a study of 3,417 adults with obesity or overweight, reporting 20.4% weight loss at 68 weeks including dropouts and 22.7% among completers. He concludes it looks better than semaglutide alone and similar to tirzepatide.

He then turns to side effects. He reports that CagriSema had the highest rates of nausea (55%) and vomiting (26.1%), compared with about 30% and 17% for semaglutide and about 23% and 9% for tirzepatide. He adds that most patients who get these effects are generally able to tolerate them and continue.

His summary is that CagriSema is among the strongest weight loss medications on current data, with nausea and vomiting as its main weakness. He notes it was not commercially available at the time of recording and that regulatory review and real world long term data are still needed.

What this video doesn’t settle

  • The video relies on cross-trial comparisons of separate studies, so it does not establish how the three drugs compare in a head-to-head trial.
  • It gives no trial names, doses for CagriSema or participant details beyond the 3,417 adults, and the figures come from on-screen graphs the speaker reads out.
  • Long term safety and real world performance of CagriSema are explicitly left open by the speaker.

Questions this video answers

What is CagriSema made of?

Dr Kidd says CagriSema combines semaglutide, a GLP1 agonist, with cagrilintide, which he describes as an amylin and calcitonin receptor agonist. He contrasts this with tirzepatide, which combines two incretin hormones.

How much weight loss did CagriSema show compared with semaglutide and tirzepatide?

Dr Kidd cites 20.4% average weight loss at 68 weeks for CagriSema (22.7% among completers). That compares with about 15% for semaglutide and 20.9% for tirzepatide. He concludes it beats semaglutide alone and looks similar to tirzepatide.

Does CagriSema cause more nausea than other GLP-1 drugs?

According to Dr Kidd, it did: 55% of patients reported nausea and 26.1% vomiting. He cites about 30% and 17% for semaglutide and about 23% and 9% for tirzepatide, and says most affected patients are generally able to continue.

Is CagriSema available to patients yet?

Dr Kidd says it was not commercially available when he recorded the video. He says regulatory review and long term real world data are still needed to confirm the findings.
